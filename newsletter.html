A Ketogenic Diet for Treatment-Resistant Depression: A Randomized Clinical Trial
JAMA Psychiatry • 2026 Feb 4 • Gao M, Kirk M, Knight H, Lash E et al. CME Clinical Trial
Oxford researchers randomized 88 patients with treatment-resistant depression to either a ketogenic diet or a control diet for 6 weeks. Both groups improved, but the ketogenic diet group showed significantly greater depression improvement. The catch: while statistically significant, the effect size was modest, and the benefit didn't clearly extend to secondary outcome measures. This is one of the first RCTs to test dietary intervention specifically in TRD, and it points toward metabolic pathways as a legitimate therapeutic target — but the authors caution that the clinical relevance is still uncertain.
Implications for practice: This isn't ready to be a standard recommendation, but it opens a conversation with patients who are treatment-resistant and interested in lifestyle approaches. The ketogenic diet may have adjunctive value for some TRD patients, but the modest effect size means it shouldn't replace pharmacotherapy. Watch for larger confirmatory trials.

Ketogenic Diets and Depression and Anxiety: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
JAMA Psychiatry • 2026 Jan 1 • Janssen-Aguilar R, Vije T, Peera M et al. CME Review
This systematic review and meta-analysis pooled available evidence on whether ketogenic diets improve depression and anxiety symptoms. The key finding: depressive symptoms did improve with ketogenic diets, particularly in studies that confirmed participants had actually achieved ketosis through biochemical verification. However, evidence for anxiety symptom improvement remained inconclusive. The analysis highlights an important methodological lesson — studies that didn't verify ketosis showed weaker effects, suggesting that actually being in ketosis (not just attempting the diet) is what matters therapeutically.
Implications for practice: If considering dietary interventions for depressed patients, the evidence favors ketogenic approaches over other dietary modifications for mood — but only if patients can maintain true ketosis. Verifying ketone levels (via blood or urine) would be essential for any clinical application. The lack of anxiety benefit means this shouldn't be positioned as a broad-spectrum lifestyle intervention for psychiatric symptoms.

Brain Neuroplasticity Mechanisms in Psychiatric Illnesses and in the Development of Novel Treatments
American Journal of Psychiatry • 2026 Feb • AJP Editorial Board CME Review
This comprehensive review examines how neuroplasticity — the brain's ability to rewire synaptic connections — underpins both the development of psychiatric disorders and the mechanisms through which treatments work. The authors argue that impaired neuroplasticity is a common thread linking depression, schizophrenia, PTSD, and anxiety disorders, and that successful treatments (from SSRIs to psychotherapy to ketamine) all share the ability to restore or enhance neural plasticity. The piece synthesizes evidence from molecular, cellular, and circuit-level studies to build a unified framework for understanding psychiatric treatment mechanisms.
Implications for practice: This review provides a useful conceptual framework for understanding why diverse treatments can work for the same condition. It reinforces that treatments enhancing neuroplasticity — including exercise, psychotherapy, and pharmacotherapy — may have synergistic effects when combined, supporting multi-modal treatment approaches.

Association Between Polygenic Risk for Major Depression and Brain Structure in a Mega-Analysis of 50,975 Participants
Molecular Psychiatry • 2026 Feb • ENIGMA MDD Working Group
The ENIGMA consortium pooled brain imaging and genetic data from nearly 51,000 participants across 11 studies to ask a straightforward question: does having a higher genetic risk for depression show up in brain structure? This is one of the largest neuroimaging genetics studies in psychiatry to date. While the specific structural correlates are still being characterized, the sheer sample size gives this analysis statistical power that previous smaller studies lacked, making it a landmark contribution to understanding the biological basis of depression risk.
Implications for practice: While not immediately practice-changing, this study strengthens the biological basis for depression as a brain disorder and may eventually support polygenic risk scores as part of risk stratification. It reinforces the message to patients and families that depression has measurable neurobiological underpinnings.

Postmarketing Safety of Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation: A 10-Year MAUDE Database Analysis of Adverse Events and Technological Advancements
The Journal of Clinical Psychiatry • 2026 Feb 11 • JCP Research Team CME Review
This study pulled a decade's worth of adverse event reports from the FDA's MAUDE database for all TMS devices — the first device-agnostic safety analysis of its kind. After deduplication, 200 unique adverse event reports were analyzed. The study characterizes patterns of reported symptoms, differences between manufacturers, device-related issues, and reporting delays. Given TMS's expanding use beyond depression (OCD, smoking cessation, PTSD), this real-world safety data fills an important postmarketing surveillance gap.
Implications for practice: The relatively small number of unique adverse events (200 over 10 years) across all TMS devices is reassuring for clinicians recommending TMS. However, be aware of reporting delays and manufacturer-specific variations when counseling patients. This data supports TMS's favorable safety profile but underscores the need for ongoing vigilance as indications expand.

Artificial Intelligence in Depression-Medication Enhancement (AID-ME): A Cluster Randomized Trial of a Deep-Learning-Enabled Clinical Decision Support System
The Journal of Clinical Psychiatry • 2025 Aug • Benrimoh D et al. CME Clinical Trial
Can an AI tool help clinicians pick the right antidepressant the first time? This cluster-randomized trial tested the Aifred Clinical Decision Support System, which uses deep learning to predict medication remission probabilities based on individual patient data. Clinicians randomized to the AI-assisted arm saw significantly improved remission rates and faster improvement compared to those using standard guidelines and questionnaires alone. The trial was conducted across diverse US and Canadian settings but was terminated early due to COVID-related funding shortfalls.
Implications for practice: While AI-guided prescribing isn't mainstream yet, this is one of the first rigorous RCTs to show it can actually improve depression outcomes in real clinical settings. Keep an eye on tools like this — personalized treatment prediction may become a standard part of prescribing workflows within the next few years, particularly for patients who haven't responded to initial treatment attempts.

New Phase 3 Data: CAPLYTA (Lumateperone) Achieves Greater Remission in Major Depressive Disorder
Press Release / Clinical Data • 2026 Jan 16 • Johnson & Johnson / Intra-Cellular Therapies CME Clinical Trial
New analysis of Phase 3 data shows that lumateperone (CAPLYTA) 42mg added to an antidepressant produced significantly higher remission rates in adults with MDD compared to placebo plus antidepressant at 6 weeks, with benefits sustained through a 6-month open-label extension. CAPLYTA is an atypical antipsychotic with a unique pharmacology — it simultaneously modulates serotonin, dopamine, and glutamate signaling. It's already FDA-approved for adjunctive MDD treatment, schizophrenia, and bipolar depression.
Implications for practice: If you have MDD patients not reaching remission on their current antidepressant, this additional remission data strengthens the case for considering CAPLYTA as an adjunctive option. Its relatively favorable metabolic profile compared to other atypical antipsychotics makes it appealing, though somnolence and sedation remain common side effects to discuss with patients.

BPL-003 (Intranasal Mebufotenin) Shows Rapid, Durable Antidepressant Effects in Treatment-Resistant Depression: Phase 2b Results
Clinical Trial Results • 2026 Jan • atai Life Sciences / Beckley Psytech CME Clinical Trial
This Phase 2b trial — the largest controlled study of mebufotenin (a tryptamine psychedelic derived from toad secretions, also known as 5-MeO-DMT) — tested a single intranasal dose in treatment-resistant depression across 38 sites in 6 countries. The results were striking: BPL-003 produced rapid, robust, and durable antidepressant effects after just one dose. Unlike psilocybin sessions that take 6-8 hours, mebufotenin's effects are brief (15-45 minutes), making it more practical for clinical settings.
Implications for practice: This is still investigational, but the practical advantages of mebufotenin over other psychedelic-assisted therapies (very short session duration, intranasal administration) could make it more scalable if approved. Watch for Phase 3 data. For now, patients asking about psychedelic treatments should know the evidence base is growing rapidly with multiple compounds in late-stage trials.

FDA Approves ProlivRx: First At-Home Neuromodulation Therapy for Treatment-Resistant Depression
FDA Approval / MOOD Trial • 2026 Jan • Neurolief Inc. CME
The FDA approved ProlivRx, the first prescription at-home brain neuromodulation device for adults with MDD who haven't responded to at least one antidepressant. The device uses external Combined Occipital and Trigeminal Afferent Stimulation (eCOT-AS) — a novel approach that stimulates multiple nerve pathways simultaneously. The approval was based on the MOOD trial, a randomized, controlled, multicenter study showing ProlivRx significantly improved remission rates with a favorable safety profile. Unlike TMS, which requires clinic visits, ProlivRx is used at home under physician direction.
Implications for practice: This is a genuinely new treatment modality for your TRD patients. The at-home format removes the logistic barrier of daily clinic visits that limits TMS access. It's worth learning more about ProlivRx's specific protocol and candidacy criteria — it could be a practical option for treatment-resistant patients who can't access TMS or don't want to try ketamine/esketamine.

ImPuls: Cost-Effective Group Exercise Intervention Improves Mental Health Across Diagnoses in Outpatient Care
The Lancet Psychiatry • 2026 Jan 27 • Lancet Psychiatry Research Group CME Clinical Trial
The ImPuls program — a structured, group-based exercise intervention designed to work across multiple psychiatric diagnoses — was evaluated as an add-on to treatment-as-usual in outpatient psychiatric care. The results showed it was both clinically effective at improving mental health outcomes and cost-effective compared to standard care alone. What makes ImPuls notable is its transdiagnostic design: rather than being tailored to depression or anxiety specifically, it works across the diagnostic spectrum, making it practical for mixed outpatient settings where patients carry varied diagnoses.
Implications for practice: This is the kind of evidence needed to justify exercise programming in outpatient psych settings. If your practice or affiliated clinic is considering adding structured exercise groups, the ImPuls model provides an evidence-based, cost-effective framework. The transdiagnostic approach means you don't need diagnosis-specific programs — one well-designed exercise group can benefit patients across your caseload.

FDA Removes Suicidal Ideation Warning from GLP-1 Receptor Agonist Labels
FDA Drug Safety Communication • 2026 Jan 13 • FDA Safety Communication CME Guideline
After a comprehensive review including meta-analyses of over 100,000 patients and large real-world studies, the FDA concluded there is no evidence linking GLP-1 receptor agonists (liraglutide/Saxenda, semaglutide/Wegovy, tirzepatide/Zepbound) to increased risk of suicidal ideation, self-harm, or other psychiatric adverse events. The agency has requested manufacturers remove the suicidal ideation/behavior risk language from product labeling. This resolves a concern that had been flagged when early adverse event reports trickled in as GLP-1 use exploded for weight management.
Implications for practice: This is reassuring for the many psychiatric patients on GLP-1 agonists for weight management — especially those on antipsychotics with metabolic side effects. You can counsel patients that the FDA has thoroughly investigated and cleared these medications of psychiatric risk. No need to avoid GLP-1 agonists in patients with mood disorders or suicidal ideation history based on this concern.

Evaluating the Effectiveness of Cyproheptadine on Acute Neuroleptic-Induced Akathisia: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology • 2026 Feb • Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology Research Group CME Clinical Trial
Akathisia remains one of the most distressing antipsychotic side effects, and current treatments (propranolol, benzodiazepines) don't always work. This double-blind RCT tested cyproheptadine — a serotonin antagonist and antihistamine already available as a generic — against placebo for acute neuroleptic-induced akathisia. Cyproheptadine significantly reduced akathisia symptoms compared to placebo, suggesting its antiserotonergic properties may counteract the serotonin-mediated component of antipsychotic-induced restlessness.
Implications for practice: This gives you another tool in the akathisia management toolbox, especially when propranolol is contraindicated (asthma, bradycardia) or benzodiazepines are undesirable. Cyproheptadine is inexpensive, widely available, and generally well-tolerated. Consider it as an alternative or adjunct for patients struggling with akathisia who haven't responded to standard approaches.

Centanafadine NDA Accepted for Priority Review for ADHD in Children, Adolescents, and Adults
FDA Regulatory Update • 2026 Jan • FDA / Otsuka CME
The FDA accepted for priority review the New Drug Application for centanafadine, a novel once-daily extended-release norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin reuptake inhibitor (NDSRI) for ADHD across all age groups. If approved, centanafadine would be the first new ADHD medication mechanism in decades — unlike existing stimulants (which primarily boost dopamine/norepinephrine) or atomoxetine (norepinephrine only), centanafadine adds serotonin reuptake inhibition, potentially offering ADHD benefit with anxiolytic and mood-stabilizing properties.
Implications for practice: This could be a significant addition to the ADHD toolbox, particularly for patients with comorbid anxiety or mood symptoms who don't tolerate stimulants well. Priority review means a decision is expected within 6-8 months. Start thinking about which patients in your practice might benefit from a triple reuptake inhibitor approach if this gains approval.

Mindfulness Meditation Side Effects: Large Study Identifies Risk Factors for Adverse Experiences
Scientific Reports / Nature • 2025 Nov • Van Dam N et al. Review
A large study found that mindfulness meditation, while broadly beneficial, can cause adverse experiences in certain individuals. People who had active mental health symptoms or psychological distress within 30 days before meditating were significantly more likely to report negative effects. Intensive residential retreats with extended silent meditation periods were also a risk factor for functional impairment. Researcher Nicholas Van Dam emphasized that the field needs better informed consent practices so participants understand meditation isn't universally benign.
Implications for practice: When recommending mindfulness to patients, consider their current symptom severity. Patients with active psychosis, severe dissociation, or acute PTSD symptoms may not be good candidates for intensive meditation practices. Recommend graduated approaches — start with brief, guided sessions rather than jumping into silent retreats. This data supports individualized recommendations rather than blanket endorsement of mindfulness for all psychiatric patients.

Efficacy and Tolerability of Seven Antipsychotic Drugs in Acutely Ill Patients With Schizophrenia: A Randomized, Multicenter, Assessor-Blinded Trial (SINO)
American Journal of Psychiatry • 2026 Feb • Zhao G, Sun Y, Zhang Y et al. CME Clinical Trial
This is the largest head-to-head antipsychotic comparison trial ever conducted — 3,067 acutely ill inpatients with schizophrenia across 32 hospitals were randomized to one of seven antipsychotics (olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, aripiprazole, ziprasidone, perphenazine, or haloperidol) for 6 weeks. Olanzapine and risperidone came out on top, showing significantly greater PANSS score improvement than aripiprazole, ziprasidone, and quetiapine, with differences of 5.5–7.9 percentage points. Notably, haloperidol and perphenazine performed comparably to olanzapine and risperidone on efficacy, though their side effect profiles differed. The study included a young population (18–45) with equal sex distribution, filling critical gaps in comparative antipsychotic data.
Implications for practice: When treating acute schizophrenia exacerbations, this trial supports olanzapine and risperidone as first choices for symptom reduction. However, the comparable efficacy of perphenazine and haloperidol is noteworthy for cost-conscious settings — the choice should be individualized based on each patient's metabolic risk, EPS susceptibility, and prior response history.

Using an emulated target trial design, researchers examined whether doxycycline exposure in adolescent psychiatric patients was associated with altered risk of developing schizophrenia. Doxycycline has known anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties beyond its antibiotic effects, and prior observational data suggested it might reduce psychosis risk. Latency to Long-Acting Injectable Antipsychotic Initiation and Psychiatric Hospitalization in First-Episode Schizophrenia
The Journal of Clinical Psychiatry • 2026 Feb • Korean HIRA Database Study Group CME Clinical Trial
Using Korea's national insurance claims database, researchers tracked 6,380 first-episode schizophrenia patients receiving LAI antipsychotics, grouping them by how long after diagnosis they started LAIs (<1 year to >5 years). A paradoxical finding emerged: while patients who started LAIs later showed better treatment adherence (possibly because they were more engaged with care by then), those who started LAIs early had significantly lower total hospitalization burden. This is one of the largest real-world studies on LAI timing in first-episode schizophrenia.
Implications for practice: This supports the growing push to offer LAIs earlier in the illness course rather than reserving them for treatment failure. When you have a first-episode patient who's stabilized, consider having the LAI conversation sooner rather than later — the hospitalization reduction benefit appears strongest with early initiation, even if patients initially seem adherent to oral medications.

The much-anticipated ARISE Phase 3 trial tested Cobenfy (xanomeline/trospium) — the first muscarinic agonist-based antipsychotic, approved in 2024 as monotherapy for schizophrenia — as an add-on to atypical antipsychotics for patients with inadequately controlled symptoms. Unfortunately, adjunctive Cobenfy did not show a statistically significant difference from placebo. However, don't expect it to work as an add-on to existing antipsychotics for partial responders. The mechanism of muscarinic agonism may not be complementary to dopamine blockade in the way that was hoped.
